Research

The approach has worked on brain function from EEG and on joint structure from high-resolution CT. It now targets cell aggregates and early embryos, whose mechanical state reports on their function and developmental potential but cannot be measured without handling or destroying them. Everything is built to be adopted, replicated and extended by other groups.

Three lines of work, in the order I took them up. All of them are still running.

  1. 2017–

    Brain activity and language

    Cranfield University, United Kingdom

    Machine-learning frameworks for diagnosing dementia from EEG, using frequency, time-frequency and connectivity features. The line continues through research projects with medical students in Australia. The latest paper, in the Journal of Neuroscience Methods (2026), shows that analysis choices alone can produce apparent rich-club differences. Thai linguistics sits on this line as well, so far as a book chapter on reported thought in Thai news reports, because the next EEG work is on language.

  2. 2019–

    Joints and mineralised tissue

    Integrative Cartilage Research Group of Kathryn Stok, The University of Melbourne, Australia

    Reproducible, high-throughput morphometry of bone, cartilage and the osteochondral interface from microCT, contrast-enhanced CT and synchrotron imaging, used to track coordinated tissue change in preclinical models of osteoarthritis. The collaboration with the University of Melbourne continues, with results from the postdoc still appearing, and I review CT imaging papers for journals and conferences.

  3. 2026–

    Cell aggregates and embryos

    Cell Mechanics Group of Jeonghyun Kim, in the Biothermal Engineering Laboratory, Kyushu University, Japan

    Estimating the mechanical properties of embryos and 3D cell aggregates non-invasively, by pairing routine microscopy with quantitative image features, machine learning and experimental mechanics. This is the main programme now, supported by a JSPS Postdoctoral Fellowship.
